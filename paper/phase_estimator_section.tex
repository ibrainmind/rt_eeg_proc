\section*{Problem Formulation (Background)}

Following repetitive-control modeling conventions, the measured ear-EEG signal is modeled as
\begin{equation}
y(t) = s(t) + d_r(t) + n(t),
\label{eq:meas}
\end{equation}
where $s(t)$ is the neural signal of interest, $d_r(t)$ is the heartbeat-synchronous repetitive 
cardiac disturbance, and $n(t)$ aggregates asynchronous interference and sensor noise. The subscript 
$r$ denotes the repetitive nature of the disturbance. Its period is heartbeat dependent and is set by 
ECG R-peak timing, with beat interval
\begin{equation}
T_k = t_{k+1} - t_k,
\label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
where $t_k$ is the $k$-th detected R-peak instant.

\begin{assumption}\label{asm:tk_slowly_varying}
The beat period $T_k$ is assumed slowly varying, dominated by respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), with
additional small modulation from low-level muscle/motion effects in resting-state recordings.
\end{assumption}

The repetitive disturbance is represented as a phase-indexed waveform
\begin{equation}
d_r(t) = d_r\!\left(\phi(t)\right), \qquad
\phi(t) = \frac{t - t_k}{T_k}, \quad t \in [t_k, t_{k+1}),
\label{eq:phase_model}
\end{equation}
with $\phi(t)\in[0,1)$ the R-peak-aligned cardiac phase.

\begin{problem}\label{prob:timing_estimation}
Estimate $\hat{t}_k$ robustly so that EEG samples can be phase-aligned via the phase model.
The estimated cardiac phase between beats advances as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\phi}(t) = \frac{t - \hat{t}_k}{\hat{T}_k}, \qquad \hat{T}_k = \hat{t}_{k+1} - \hat{t}_k,
\label{eq:phi_hat}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{T}_k$ is the estimated beat period.
\end{problem}

\begin{noteenv}\label{note:pll_prediction}
Since $T_k$ is slowly varying (Assumption~\ref{asm:tk_slowly_varying}), $\hat{T}_k$ can be reliably
predicted between successive R-peaks. The PI tracker advances phase continuously between beats so
that $\hat{\phi}(t)$ remains well-defined in real-time causal operation.
\end{noteenv}

\section{ECG Phase Estimator}
\label{sec:ecg_phase_estimator}

\subsection{Min/max tracker + PI/PID phase tracking}

A QRS-emphasis band-pass feeds two streaming trackers: a peak tracker $pk(t)$ (fast attack, slow 
release) and a baseline tracker $bl(t)$ (slow moving average). The normalized gate
\begin{equation}
g(t) = \frac{x(t) - bl(t)}{pk(t) - bl(t)}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
is amplitude-drift invariant, so posture/contact drift does not defeat detection. A Schmitt 
hysteresis and a 250~ms refractory yield clean beat gating; the fiducial $t_k$ is the raw-reference 
maximum within the gated window (low jitter).

A PI phase-locked loop converts sparse fiducials into a continuous phase. Between beats the phase 
advances by the instantaneous rate estimate,
\begin{equation}
p(t+1) = \big[\, p(t) + \mathrm{fr}(t) \,\big] \bmod 1, \qquad \mathrm{fr}(t) = 
\frac{1}{\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}(t)\, f_s},
\label{eq:phaseacc}
\end{equation}
where $f_s$ is the sample rate. At each fiducial, a proportional term realigns phase and an 
integral term updates the rate estimate,
\begin{align}
p &\leftarrow p - K_p \, e_p, \label{eq:pcorr}\\
\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}} &\leftarrow \mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}} + K_i 
\,(\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meas}} - \mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}), \label{eq:rcorr}
\end{align}
with $e_p$ the wrapped phase error and $K_p,K_i$ the PI gains. The loop bandwidth is set above 
respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA, $\sim$0.2--0.4~Hz) and below per-beat detection noise. An 
arrhythmia supervisor classifies each beat by the ratio 
$\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meas}}/\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}$ and applies the matching 
reflex---\emph{premature}: trust the measured phase, freeze the rate and learning; \emph{missed}: 
trust the prediction, keep the rate---protecting both the loop and the template.

\subsection{Algorithm summary}

Per sample: (1)~update trackers \eqref{eq:gate} and gate; (2)~advance phase \eqref{eq:phaseacc}, 
correct at fiducials \eqref{eq:pcorr}--\eqref{eq:rcorr}, run the supervisor; (3)~ILC
lookup, limited subtraction, and template update on normal beats. The proposed online path is
therefore constant-time per sample, with template memory 
linear in the window length.

\section{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity_analysis}

Let $L_w$ be the template length and $C$ the channel count. The proposed phase-tracker + ILC path 
is lightweight: tracker updates and gating are constant-time, PI/PID phase update is constant-time 
(amortized), and ILC lookup/update is constant-time with memory $L_w$. Thus, per-sample complexity 
is $O(1)$ and memory is $O(L_w)$ for each channel. This profile is suitable for always-on 
low-power ear-EEG devices. For fairness, baseline adaptive cancellers (e.g., LMS/RLS) are reported 
separately in experiments and are not part of the proposed online deployment path.

\subsection{Real ear-EEG validation: coherence and repeatable-template evidence}
\label{sec:realdata}
To test the coherence assumption behind reference-driven cancellers and confirm that a genuine
repeatable cardiac template exists outside simulation, we analyze a 10-minute segment (717 beats;
2 premature, 0 missed) from the SeizeIT2 dataset \cite{seizeit2}, which pairs a wearable
behind-the-ear EEG channel (256~Hz) with a synchronized chest ECG lead during video-EEG epilepsy
monitoring.

\emph{Coherence is near zero, even under best-case R-locked pooling.} Fig.~\ref{fig:coh}(a) shows
magnitude-squared coherence between the ECG reference and the ear-EEG channel from long, many-average
Welch segments across 0--60~Hz: coherence averages $6\times10^{-10}$ over 8--20~Hz and is visually
indistinguishable from zero across the whole spectrum -- inconsistent with any stationary linear
channel between $r(t)$ and $d(t)$. A skeptic could object that this washes out a relationship
holding only locally around each beat; pooling short-time estimates over rolling 12-beat R-aligned
batches (715 estimates, Fig.~\ref{fig:coh}(b)) gives the same answer -- coherence stays low and
stable ($0.06$--$0.17$, mean $0.11$) throughout the recording, an order of magnitude below what a
usable Hammerstein/RLS reference-driven canceller would need. Even granting the reference every
possible timing advantage, the ear-EEG artifact waveform is not linearly predictable from the ECG
waveform.

\emph{A repeatable, R-anchored template nonetheless exists.} Coherence measures waveform
predictability from the reference, not repeatability across beats. Non-causal ILC/synchronous
averaging (\S IV-B) applied directly to the real ear-EEG channel (Fig.~\ref{fig:real_causal_buffered})
recovers a clear, consistent deflection time-locked to the ECG R-peak, confirming the cardiac field
artifact at the ear is genuinely timing-repeatable -- exactly the property ILC exploits -- even
though it fails the coherence test above. This is the real-data analogue of synchronous-average
disk-servo RRO/NRRO separation: only the R-peak \emph{instant} is used, never the ECG waveform shape.
The causal-vs-buffered R-spike tradeoff is likewise real, not a simulation artifact: buffered matches
non-causal (R-spike peak-to-peak $5.02\times10^{-5}$~V vs.\ $5.07\times10^{-5}$~V), while the causal
template is capped by RR-prediction error to $3.47\times10^{-5}$~V, a $32\%$ reduction, consistent
with \S IV-B. Cardiac-band (1--20~Hz) power is reduced by $0.9$/$0.8$/$0.4$~dB for
non-causal/buffered/causal respectively; the modest magnitude versus the semi-synthetic ceiling
(Table~\ref{tab:results}) reflects broadband physiological noise at this electrode and that only the
beat-locked component is addressable by ILC by construction.

These observations are the empirical basis for this paper's scope: a null coherence result that
rules out a reference-driven residual stage as a primary contribution here, and a positive
repeatability result that justifies keeping the timing-driven ILC stage. The linear RLS canceller in
Table~\ref{tab:results} is retained only as a semi-synthetic baseline, evaluated on data explicitly
given a known Hammerstein/volume-conduction channel between $r(t)$ and $d(t)$; it is not part of the
proposed real-time deployment path.

\subsection{Semi-synthetic data}
The real-data evidence above has no ground truth $s(t)$, so we complement it with a semi-synthetic
protocol that provides exact-reference SNR metrics; the ILC-driven design validated qualitatively
above is here validated quantitatively. Following the semi-synthetic protocol common in EEG denoising \cite{zhang2021}, a low-CFA neural
surrogate (pink noise plus an amplitude-modulated 10~Hz alpha rhythm) provides ground truth $s(t)$. 
A synthetic ECG source $u(t)$ with RSA-modulated RR intervals is converted into a beat-locked 
artifact template with slow amplitude and morphology drift, then added at a controlled input SNR; 
one premature and one dropped beat are injected. This yields exact-reference metrics without manual 
labeling. Metrics: artifact SNR (steady state), spectral cardiac-peak reduction, and alpha 
preservation.

\subsection{Baselines}
Within the semi-synthetic protocol only (\S\ref{sec:realdata} shows why a reference-driven residual
stage cannot contribute on the real recording), we compare against (i)~a linear RLS/LMS
ECG-reference canceller \cite{widrow1975,haykin2014,sayed2008}; (ii)~an EKF/state-space canceller
\emph{[placeholder---to be added]} \cite{sameni2007}; and (iii)~low-channel ICA
\emph{[placeholder---to be added]} \cite{jung2000}, which is expected to degrade at $C<8$.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports steady-state artifact SNR for the main approaches, generated

\subsection{R-peak prediction accuracy on real ECG}
\label{sec:rpeak_prediction}

The claim above---that strict-causal ILC is limited by RR prediction rather than by the
template---is testable directly on the phase estimator, without involving the canceller at all. We
run it on the chest ECG of sub-070, ses-01 (\rpNrunsAll{} runs, first \rpDur{}~s of each;
\rpNruns{} kept after dropping runs whose mean RR falls below $500$~ms, which is detector doubling
rather than genuine tachycardia), and compare the two anchors a canceller can use.

\emph{The one-beat-delay anchor is exact, and available sooner than a beat.} The estimator sets the
fiducial $t_k$ to the argmax of the raw ECG inside the armed window, and that sample coincides with
the local $|\mathrm{ECG}|$ maximum in a $\pm 60$~ms neighbourhood on essentially every beat (median
offset \rpFidOff{}~ms); sub-sample parabolic refinement moves it by only \rpSub{}~ms, so this anchor
is quantisation-limited, not algorithm-limited. It is also declared quickly: the median detection
latency is \rpLat{}~ms (p90 \rpLatPninety{}~ms), against a mean beat interval of \rpRR{}~ms. The
full beat of latency in the buffered mode is therefore not needed to \emph{locate} the R-peak---it
is needed to measure $T_k$ and to have the template updated.

\emph{The predicted anchor is unbiased but scatters by tens of milliseconds.} At sample $n$ the
strict-causal canceller believes the R-peak occurred $\phi\hat{T}$ ago, so its predicted R-peak is
displaced from the true one by $-\mathrm{wrap}(\phi(t_k))\,\hat{T}$, which is the loop's own phase
error expressed in time. Table~\ref{tab:rpeak} reports it, alongside a re-anchored predictor
$\hat{t}_k = t_{k-1} + \hat{T}$ that uses the last detected fiducial instead of free-running phase.

\input{tab_rpeak_prediction}

The loop is genuinely locked---the signed error is \rpPllBias{}~ms, not a standing offset---but its
spread is \rpPllMad{}~ms MAD, with a p90 absolute error of \rpPllPninety{}~ms and only
\rpPllWTen\% of beats landing within $\pm 10$~ms. That is wide compared with the QRS deflection
the template must line up with, which is exactly why strict-causal cancellation degrades around the
R spike while the buffered mode does not.

\emph{Half of that scatter is a design choice, not physics.} Re-anchoring on the last fiducial cuts
the error from \rpPllMad{} to \rpOlMad{}~ms MAD and raises the $\pm 10$~ms hit rate from
\rpPllWTen\% to \rpOlWTen\%. Beyond that lies a genuine floor: one-step-ahead RR prediction on
this subject has SD \rpFloor{}~ms for a random-walk predictor (previous RR), \rpEwma{}~ms for the
EWMA the loop uses, and \rpMeanPred{}~ms for a constant mean, so RR here is closer to a random walk
than to a stationary process and the re-anchored predictor is already near what past beat timing
can deliver. Reducing it further requires information outside the beat train---respiration, since
most of this variability is RSA.

On the record used for Fig.~1 of the manuscript (run-\rpHlRun{}, \rpHlBeats{} scored beats, RR
\rpHlRR{}$\pm$\rpHlRRsd{}~ms) the same pattern holds: detection latency \rpHlLat{}~ms, PLL

a one-beat-delay anchor error of zero by construction. The practical reading is that the buffered
mode should be the default, and that a zero-latency mode is worth building only together with a
better RR predictor, with its residual timing error budgeted against the width of the feature being
cancelled.

\subsection{Against a non-causal reference, and what the loop shape says}
\label{sec:rpeak_bode}

The comparison in \S\ref{sec:rpeak_prediction} used the streaming detector's own fiducial as the
yardstick, which is itself causal. We now measure against a genuinely non-causal reference: the
whole ECG is filtered with zero phase (\texttt{filtfilt}, 8--20~Hz), peaks are taken on the
envelope, and each is refined to the local $|\mathrm{ECG}|$ extremum and then to sub-sample
resolution by parabolic fit. Of \rbNrunsAll{} runs, \rbNruns{} are kept after requiring that at
least 95\% of streaming fiducials match a reference peak; the two rejected runs are the ones whose
detector is doubling.

\emph{The detected fiducial is exact to the grid.} It matches the non-causal peak on
\rbMatch{}\% of beats with a jitter of \rbFidJitter{}~ms about its own median. Some records carry a
constant offset of tens of milliseconds, where the armed window closes before the broadest
deflection; that offset is common to every anchoring scheme---the template absorbs it---so all
figures below are reported about the median. \emph{The strict-causal predicted R-peak}, measured
against the same reference, has SD \rbPllSd{}~ms with a p90 absolute error of \rbPllPninety{}~ms,
against \rbOlSd{}~ms for the re-anchored predictor.

\subsubsection*{Beat-domain error dynamics}

Why the loop should behave that way follows from writing it in the beat domain, where it is linear
in the timing error. The period estimator is an EWMA of gain $\alpha$, whose one-step prediction
error is a discrete differentiator behind a lag,
\begin{equation}
\frac{(\hat{T}-T)(z)}{T(z)} \;=\; \frac{z^{-1}-1}{1-(1-\alpha)z^{-1}},
\label{eq:ewma}
\end{equation}
so a constant heart rate is tracked exactly and variation is not. The phase corrector removes only
$K_p$ of the residual timing error per beat, which places a leaky integrator of DC gain $1/K_p$ in
series. The strict-causal anchor therefore sees
\begin{equation}
S(z) \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1-(1-K_p)z^{-1}}}_{\text{phase corrector}}\;
\underbrace{\frac{z^{-1}-1}{1-(1-\alpha)z^{-1}}}_{\text{period estimator}},
\qquad
|S(\omega)| \;\xrightarrow[\omega\to 0]{}\; \frac{\omega}{\alpha K_p}.
\label{eq:S}
\end{equation}
The low-frequency asymptote is the design statement: the timing error scales with the
\emph{reciprocal of the product} of the two gains. As shipped both are $0.30$, so slow RR variation
is amplified by $1/(\alpha K_p) \approx 11$ relative to a loop that corrects fully and does not
smooth.

\emph{The model is validated, not assumed.} Driving Eq.~\eqref{eq:S} with each record's measured RR
sequence and taking the SD of the output predicts the observed error to within \rbModelErrPct{}\%
(median over runs; Fig.~\ref{fig:rpeakbode}(b)). Intervals the arrhythmia supervisor would reject
are replaced by the running median before filtering, so the model is driven by the same beats the
measurement is scored on.

\subsubsection*{Where the improvement is}

The measured RR spectrum peaks at \rbRRpeakCpb{} cycles per beat (\rbRRpeakHz{}~Hz), i.e.\ slow
drift rather than a respiratory line, and it is broadband. That has three consequences, all visible
in Fig.~\ref{fig:rpeakbode}.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Raise $K_p$ to 1}---anchor on the last detected fiducial instead of free-running
phase. This removes the $1/K_p$ integrator entirely and takes the predicted error from
\rbModelAsBuilt{} to \rbModelReanch{}~ms. It costs nothing: the fiducial is already available
\rpLat{}~ms after the beat.
\item \textbf{Raise $\alpha$ to 1}---predict the next interval as the previous one. Taking
\rbModelReanch{} to \rbModelLastRR{}~ms. Fig.~\ref{fig:rpeakbode}(c) shows the curve is monotone in
$\alpha$ with no interior optimum, which is the signature of an RR sequence with little
beat-to-beat predictable structure: smoothing it only adds lag.
\item \textbf{Nothing more elaborate helps.} A type-2 $\alpha$-$\beta$ tracker that also carries a
period rate gives \rbModelAB{}~ms, and a resonator placed at the dominant RR line gives
\rbModelRes{}~ms---neither beats the deadbeat predictor, because the disturbance is broadband and
has no line to notch. The $\alpha$-$\beta$ family is also a trap here: at $a_2=0$ its denominator
factors as $(z-1)(z-(1-a_1))$, a pole on the unit circle for any $a_1$, so a naive gain sweep
reports its best score at a marginally stable design.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, the strict-causal anchor can be improved roughly threefold, from \rbModelAsBuilt{}
to \rbModelLastRR{}~ms SD, by changing two gains rather than by adding machinery. On the record used
for Fig.~1 of the manuscript (run-\rbHlRun{}) the same three steps read \rbHlPll{}~ms measured
(\rbHlModel{}~ms modelled), \rbHlOl{}~ms re-anchored, and \rbHlPrev{}~ms with the deadbeat
predictor, against a fiducial jitter of \rbHlFidJit{}~ms for the one-beat-delay anchor. The residual
is then set by the part of the next RR interval that the past genuinely does not determine, and
closing that gap needs a signal outside the beat train---respiration being the obvious candidate.

\subsection{Designing the strict-causal predictor: a worked PI procedure}
\label{sec:pll_design}

\S\ref{sec:rpeak_bode} showed \emph{that} the loop can be improved and by roughly how much. This
section is the procedure that gets there without a gain sweep: identify the RR process, let the
disturbance model fix the loop order, and set the gains from two measured variances. The worked
numbers are sub-070, ses-01, \pdNruns{} runs.

\subsubsection*{Step 1: state the estimation problem}

At zero latency the canceller needs $t_k$ before it happens. Take the beat instant as position and
the period as velocity,
\begin{equation}
t_k = t_{k-1} + T_k,
\qquad
T_k = T_{k-1} + w_k,
\qquad
z_k = t_k + v_k,
\label{eq:statemodel}
\end{equation}
with $w_k$ the period process noise (how much the heart rate wanders per beat) and $v_k$ the error
with which the detector places the fiducial. Everything below follows from the ratio of those two.

\subsubsection*{Step 2: identify the RR process}

Plot the sample autocorrelation of $T_k$ and of its first difference
(Fig.~\ref{fig:plldesign}(a)). Three outcomes matter:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item $T_k$ uncorrelated $\Rightarrow$ stationary about a mean; predict with the mean, and no
integral action is needed.
\item $\Delta T_k$ uncorrelated $\Rightarrow$ random walk; the previous interval is the optimal
predictor and one integrator suffices.
\item $\rho_1(\Delta T) < 0$ $\Rightarrow$ local level (random walk plus white measurement noise);
exponential smoothing is optimal and $\alpha^{*}=1-\theta$ follows in closed form from
$\rho_1 = -\theta/(1+\theta^{2})$.
\end{itemize}

Here the ACF of $T_k$ decays slowly---the series is integrated, not stationary---while
$\rho_1(\Delta T) = \pdRho{}$, \emph{positive}. That rules out the local-level model and says the
rate is \emph{smoother} than a random walk: consecutive increments share a sign, as a slow
respiratory or thermoregulatory drift would produce. Two consequences follow immediately. A loop
with no integral action cannot track this disturbance at all, and a random-walk process model is
conservative rather than wrong, so gains derived from it will be slightly hot.

\subsubsection*{Step 3: let the disturbance fix the loop order}

By the internal model principle the loop must contain a model of what it has to reject. An
integrated rate needs one integrator in the period path; that integrator is exactly the ``I'' of a
PI phase-locked loop. Writing the loop in the beat domain,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{t}_k^{-} &= \hat{t}_{k-1} + \hat{T}_{k-1}, &\qquad
r_k &= z_k - \hat{t}_k^{-},\\
\hat{t}_k &= \hat{t}_k^{-} + a\,r_k, &\qquad
\hat{T}_k &= \hat{T}_{k-1} + b\,r_k \;(+\,c\,(r_k-r_{k-1})),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:piloop}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the proportional (phase) gain, $b$ the integral (period) gain and $c$ an optional
derivative term. Equation~\eqref{eq:piloop} is simultaneously a PI PLL and an $\alpha$--$\beta$
tracker; the published anchor is $\hat{t}_k^{-}$ and the quantity to minimise is
$e_k = \hat{t}_k^{-} - t_k$. The shipped estimator is \emph{not} of this form: it pulls the phase
proportionally and estimates the period by a separate EWMA of measured intervals, which is why it
behaves like a loop with no integrator in the right place.

\subsubsection*{Step 4: set the gains from the two variances}

Estimate the process noise as the beat-to-beat interval increment, $\sigma_w = \mathrm{SD}(\Delta
T) = \pdSigmaW{}$~ms, and the measurement noise as the fiducial jitter measured in
\S\ref{sec:rpeak_bode}, $\sigma_v = \pdSigmaV{}$~ms. Their ratio is the tracking index
\begin{equation}
\Lambda = \frac{\sigma_w T^{2}}{\sigma_v} = \pdLambda{} \quad (T = 1 \text{ beat}),
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
and the steady-state gains follow in closed form from Kalata's steady-state solution for the tracking index:
\begin{equation}
r = \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(4+\Lambda-\sqrt{8\Lambda+\Lambda^{2}}\bigr),
\qquad
a = 1-r^{2},
\qquad
b = 2(2-a)-4\sqrt{1-a},
\label{eq:kalata}
\end{equation}
giving $a=\pdKalA{}$, $b=\pdKalB{}$. Driving Eq.~\eqref{eq:piloop} with the measured beat trains at
those gains yields \pdKalSd{}~ms, against \pdOptSd{}~ms for the best point of an exhaustive
$(a,b)$ grid at $(\pdOptA{},\pdOptB{})$: the closed form lands within $0.4$~ms of a search, from two
variances and no tuning. The interpretation of $\Lambda = \pdLambda{}$ is that the fiducial is far
quieter than the heart rate is variable, so the loop should be fast and trust each measurement
almost completely---which is why $a$ comes out near unity.

\subsubsection*{Step 5: check margin, then validate}

The $\alpha$--$\beta$ stability condition is $0<a<2$, $0<b<4-2a$, drawn in
Fig.~\ref{fig:plldesign}(b). The optimum sits in a long shallow valley with a cliff just beyond it,
so the sensible choice is to back off along the valley rather than sit at the minimum: $(1.0,1.2)$
costs about $0.2$~ms against $(\pdOptA{},\pdOptB{})$ and buys real margin. Validation against the
shipped loop is then Fig.~\ref{fig:plldesign}(c): \pdBuilt{}~ms as built, \pdOptSd{}~ms designed.

\subsubsection*{What the exercise actually shows}

\emph{It is the integral term that matters, not the proportional one.} With the period estimate
frozen, a proportional-only loop is stuck at \pdPonly{}~ms however its gain is chosen---barely
better than the shipped \pdBuilt{}~ms---because no amount of phase pulling lets a loop follow a rate
it is not tracking. Adding integral action takes it to \pdOptSd{}~ms.

\emph{Derivative action buys nothing here.} The best PID over the same grid sits at
$c=\pdPidC{}$ with \pdPidSd{}~ms, indistinguishable from PI. The positive $\rho_1(\Delta T)$ says
there \emph{is} a little exploitable trend, but not enough to survive differentiating a signal whose
increments are only weakly correlated.

\emph{Two caveats before adopting the numbers.} The gains were identified on the same records they
were scored on, so \pdOptSd{}~ms is a design estimate rather than a held-out prediction; the
structural conclusions---integral action essential, derivative useless, $a$ near unity---are what
should carry over. And $\sigma_w$ varies by a factor of two across runs, so $\Lambda$ and hence the
gains are properly a running estimate, from an online $\mathrm{SD}(\Delta T)$ and fiducial jitter,
rather than constants. Ectopic beats are outside the Gaussian model entirely: the arrhythmia
supervisor still has to freeze the update on them, and no choice of $(a,b)$ substitutes for that.
