\begin{abstract}
Continuous wearable EEG is increasingly paired with consumer wearables that provide a 
heartbeat stream (e.g., smartwatch ECG/PPG). Given the available heartbeat timing reference, we propose a 
repetitive-control framework that models Cardiac Field Artifact (CFA) as a heartbeat-aligned periodic 
disturbance, and we adopt iterative learning control (ILC) to clean up the artifact in a streaming fashion.
After the mapping of measured EEG samples to the corresponding normalized cardiac phase around ECG R-peaks,
phase-synchronous averaging then preserves the heartbeat-correlated component as a model of repetitive CFA disturbances. 
We propose an ILC based approach to track the averaged repetitive disturbance  exponentially which enables the real-time CFA cancellation from 
EEG streaming. The effectiveness of proposed ILC-based CFA removal is demonstrated by the earEEG dataset using only 
the locked R-peak timing without the synchronized ECG waveforms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Wearable EEG is moving toward continuous, day-scale monitoring, and many users already wear a second 
device that provides heartbeat timing from ECG or PPG. This creates a practical path for low-channel 
ear-EEG denoising: use heartbeat timing already available from the wearable ecosystem rather than 
requiring additional EEG channels. In ear-centered montages, cardiac field artifact (CFA) is often 
prominent because electrodes are close to neck and vascular pathways. As one of the primary contaminations in wearable ear-EEG, 
CFA is the volume-conducted 
electrical field of the heart \cite{dirlich1997}, distinct from the scanner-related 
ballistocardiogram artifact emphasized in EEG-fMRI work \cite{niazy2005}. 

Classical ECG-referenced adaptive cancellation is well established \cite{widrow1975,haykin2014,sayed2008}, and it is a 
natural baseline. In our target regime it faces three practical limits: earables are 
channel-starved for blind source separation \cite{jung2000}, learned denoisers are benchmarked mainly on
high-density montages \cite{zhang2021}, and---most restrictive for a wearable deployment---reference-driven
cancellation must be driven by the ECG \emph{waveform}, sample-aligned to the EEG. Beat timing, by
contrast, is a single scalar per cardiac cycle that crosses the device boundary intact, and the
artifact remains phase-locked to it. This motivates a timing-only control view: model cardiac contamination as a repetitive disturbance and 
reject it by heartbeat phase indexing. Repetitive control and iterative learning control (ILC) are 
mature tools for periodic disturbance rejection in mechatronics \cite{hara1988}, 
and they map naturally to the heartbeat cycle when reliable beat timing is available. In this work, 
we focus on phase-aligned template learning/cancellation driven by heartbeat timing in real time streaming EEG scenarios,
especially in the low channel setup. The resulting contribution is a compact, wearable-oriented CFA removal scheme aligned with realistic 
device constraints: low channel count, low compute budget, and timing references that may come from a 
separate wearable.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

Following repetitive-control modeling conventions \cite{hara1988}\cite{longman2000}, we model 
the measured ear-EEG signal as
\begin{equation}
y(t) = s(t) + d_r(t) + n(t),
\label{eq:meas}
\end{equation}
where $s(t)$ is the neural signal of interest, $d_r(t)$ is the heartbeat-synchronous repetitive 
cardiac disturbance, and $n(t)$ aggregates asynchronous interference and sensor noise. Explicit
generative models of the ECG waveform itself \cite{sameni2007} are deliberately not required here. The subscript 
$r$ denotes the repetitive nature of the disturbance. Its period is heartbeat dependent and is set by 
ECG R-peak timing, with beat interval
\begin{equation}
T_k = t_{k} - t_{k-1},
\label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
where $t_k$ is the $k$-th detected R-peak instant.

\begin{assumption}\label{asm:cfa_dominant}
In ear-EEG, CFA is dominated by the repetitive component $d_r(t)$. The R-peak
phase-asynchronous disturbances and noise $n(t)$ are assumed zero-mean under the R-peak-synchronous averaging, and DC offset and slow drift are removed
by preprocessing.
\end{assumption}

Using heartbeat phase, we represent the repetitive disturbance as a phase-indexed waveform
\begin{equation}
d_r(t) = d_r\!\left(\phi(t)\right), \qquad
\phi(t) = \frac{t - t_k}{T_{k}}, \quad t \in [t_k, t_{k+1}),
\label{eq:phase_model}
\end{equation}
with $\phi(t)\in[0,1)$ the R-peak-aligned cardiac phase. 

\begin{assumption}\label{asm:neural_not_phase_locked}
We assume the neural signal can be decomposed as $s(t)=s_r(t)+s_n(t)$, where $s_r(t)$ is the
heartbeat-repetitive neural component and $s_n(t)$ is the non-repetitive neural component of interest.
The targeted applications in this paper do not seek to recover $s_r(t)$, so it is dropped from the
problem formulation, then we obtain $\frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{k=1}^{N_r} s_k(\phi) =0$ for large enough $N_r$ of R-peaks.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{asm:rpeak_T_varying}\label{asm:tk_slowly_varying}
The beat period $T_k$ is assumed to be accurately locked, and slowly varying due to respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), with
additional small modulation from low-level muscle/motion effects in resting-state recordings.
\end{assumption}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:cfa_removal}
Find the repetitive cardiac artifact template $\hat{d}_r(\phi(t))$ such that subtracting it from
the measured signal recovers the non-repetitive neural signal of interest, after dropping the
out-of-scope repetitive neural component $s_r(t)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}_n(t) = y(t) - \hat{d}_r\!\left(\phi(t)\right) \approx s_n(t).
\label{eq:cfa_goal}
\end{equation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:cfa_dominant}--\ref{asm:rpeak_T_varying}, $\hat{d}_r(\phi(t))$ can be
estimated from the R-peak-locked structure of $y(t)$ alone.
\end{problem}

\begin{noteenv}\label{note:timing_only}
Since $d_r(\phi(t))$ is periodic in the cardiac phase $\phi(t)$ indexed solely by R-peak instants
$\{t_k\}$, Problem~\ref{prob:cfa_removal} depends only on R-peak \emph{timing} and never on the
ECG waveform shape. Consequently, any wearable heartbeat source---smartwatch ECG, wrist
PPG, or any device delivering beat timing via a synchronization mechanism---is sufficient to
instantiate the solution.
\end{noteenv}

\begin{noteenv}\label{note:hep}
In reality, the out-of-scope $s_r(t)$ may contain a phase-synchronous component, e.g. the
heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP)---a genuine cortical response time-locked to the cardiac cycle
\cite{park2019}. This component may be absorbed into $\hat{d}_r$; preserving it is a meaningful
extension worthy of further investigation.
\end{noteenv}



To instantiate Problem~\ref{prob:cfa_removal}, let $\hat{t}_k$ be
the estimated $k$-th R-peak instant, $\hat{T}_k = \hat{t}_{k} - t_{k-1}$. Retaining the beat duration
$T_k$, we map the $k$-th beat from time relative to its R-peak into normalized
phase, with the R-peak placed at the \emph{center} of the phase support rather than at its edge, set 
$\phi\in[-c,\,1-c), c \in (0, 1)$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
d_r^{(k)}(\phi) \triangleq
\begin{cases}
d_r\!\left(\hat{t}_k + \hat{\phi}\,\hat{T}_k\right), & \hat{\phi}\in\left[-c,\,0\right),\\[3pt]
d_r\!\left(t_k + \phi\,T_k\right),   & \phi\in\left[0,\,1 - c\right),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dk_def}
\end{equation}

\begin{noteenv}\label{note:phase_origin}
For strict real-time scenarios, Eq.~(\ref{eq:dk_def}) is non-causal before the $t_k$ arrival, therefore the 
$\hat{t}_k, \hat{T}_k$ and $\hat{\phi}$ have to be predicted. If the application allows one heart beat delay that 
Eq.~(\ref{eq:dk_def}) can be applied causally with arrived and locked $t_k$ beats and no estimated notation is needed.
A forward-weighted split such as $\phi\in[-0.3,\,0.7)$ as $c=0.3$ might be more suitable for capturing the asymmetry of the cardiac cycle.
We treat $c$ as an implementation parameter. 
\end{noteenv}

\begin{noteenv}\label{note:template_window_choice}
For offline non-causal processing, assuming R-peaks are accurately detected and bad beats are rejected, we average beats on a common
normalized phase grid across $N_r$ beats:
\begin{equation}
\dhat_r(\phi) = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{k=1}^{N_r} y_k(\phi),
\qquad
y_k(\phi) \equiv y\!\left(\hat t_k + \phi T_k\right).
\label{eq:est}
\end{equation}
Substituting the signal $y_k(\phi)$ by its definition from the discrete time version of Eq.~(\ref{eq:meas}), we obtain
$\dhat_r(\phi) \equiv d_r(\phi)$, as $s_{n,k}(\phi)$ and $n_k(\phi)$ are averaged to zero over time.
For more detailed analysis, we can refer to the established template-based BCG/CFA artifact-removal
idea of \cite{allen1998}, and we will focus on real-time solution of Problem~\ref{prob:cfa_removal} in the next sections.
\end{noteenv}

\section{Repetitive Disturbance Rejection}
\label{sec:noncausal_repetitive_rejection}



\subsection{Buffered Template Averaging}





For streaming template learning, the batch average of Eq.~\eqref{eq:est} is replaced by its standard
exponentially-weighted ILC recursion, updated once per learnable beat at each phase $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{d}_r^{(k)}(\phi) = \hat{d}_r^{(k-1)}(\phi) + a\,\bigl[\,y_k(\phi) - \hat{d}_r^{(k-1)}(\phi)\,\bigr],
\label{eq:causal_ilc_update}
\end{equation}
where $a\in(0,1]$ is a tunable learning gain. The supervisor freezes the update on
premature/missed beats.
\begin{noteenv}
  In streaming operation, the averaging in Eq.~\eqref{eq:causal_ilc_update} has to
run with one beat buffered, as in strict real-time the $k$ R-peak has not arrived yet.
Once the averaging converges, this ILC recursion converges to the exponentially averaged normalized-phase template of Ref.~\cite{allen1998}.
\end{noteenv}

\subsection{Real-Time Cancellation}
\label{sec:real_time}

For applications where one beat of delay is acceptable, buffered cancellation is a strong candidate:
when the current R-peak arrives, the preceding learnable beat is cancelled using the template available
before that beat, and is then used to update the template for the following beat. The completed beat is
anchored to its detected R-peak, so there is no anchor-prediction error; its cleaned output is emitted
with one heartbeat of latency:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{s}_{k}(\phi) \approx y_{k}(\phi) - \hat{d}_r^{(k)}(\phi).
\label{eq:causal_ilc_buffered}
\end{equation}
The equation is timestamp-aligned for comparison: in a streaming implementation, the cleaned samples
of beat $k$ are emitted from a buffer only when $t_{k+1}$ arrives.
The buffered mode is therefore preferred whenever the application can tolerate one-beat delay.

\subsection{Strict real-time cancellation.}
\label{sec:strict_real_time}
When zero added latency is required, we set up the strict real-time cancellation at current $t$, anchored 
on the the next incoming R-peak at $t_{k+1}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}(t) = y(t) - \hat{d}^{(k)}_r\!\left(\hat{\phi}^{(k+1)}(t)\right).
\label{eq:strict_cancel}
\end{equation}
\begin{noteenv}
  In Eq.~(\ref{eq:strict_cancel}), we only need to run predicted cancellation for $t\in[\hat{t}_{k+1}-c\hat{T}_{k+1},\,\hat{t}_{k+1})$
  before the $t_{k+1}$ arrives, when the $t_{k+1}$ arrives, it can run the cancellation
  based on converged $\hat{d}^{(k)}_r$, which we denote the $k$-th $\hat{d}_r$ as one beat delayed template. $\hat{d}$ includes a phase de-normalization
  to be subtracted from the signal $y(t)$, we omit the notation for simplicity.

\end{noteenv}
For predicting $\hat{\phi}(t)$ for $t \in[t_{k+1} - cT_k,t_{k+1})$, we define
the observed interval $z_{k}$ as $z_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}$, and we assume the measurement model as
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:rr_model}
\begin{align}
T_{k+1} &= T_{k}+w_{k+1}, \qquad w_{k+1}\sim(0,q),
\label{eq:rr_state}\\
z_{k} &= T_{k}+v_{k}, \qquad v_{k}\sim(0,r).
\label{eq:rr_measurement}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here $q$ is the beat-to-beat RR-increment variance, and
$r$ is the R-peak timing-jitter variance. We set up the PI tracker as:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:pi_tracker}
\begin{align}
\frac{d\hat\phi}{dt}=\frac{1}{\hat{T}_{k}},
\label{eq:pi_phase_advance}\\
\hat\phi\leftarrow\hat\phi-K_p e_{\phi,k},
\label{eq:pi_onset}\\
e_{\phi,k}=\frac{z_{k}-\hat{T}_{k}}{\hat{T}_{k}},
\label{eq:pi_phase_error}\\
\hat{T}_{k+1}\leftarrow\hat{T}_{k}+K_{\mathrm{ss}}\left(z_{k}-\hat{T}_{k}\right).
\label{eq:pi_rate}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\begin{noteenv}\label{note:innovation_gate}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:pi_tracker}) provides a basic framework for predicting the $\phi(t)$ for the $k+1$ peak cancellation based PI tracking
before the arrival of the $t_{k+1}$ peak. Eq.~(\ref{eq:pi_rate}) is a first order tracking of $\hat{T}_{k+1}$ and the gain
$K_{\mathrm{ss}}$ can be obtained by scalar Kalman filter with pre-calibrated $q$ and $r$ as leaky I control, and $k_p$
applied in Eq.~(\ref{eq:pi_onset}) acts as the P control on the actual phase index. Higher order tracking algorithm could be designed
for certain application needs, and when $k_p$ and $K_{\mathrm{ss}}$ set to zero, it will fall back to re-use previous R-peak anchored phase.
\end{noteenv}
\begin{noteenv}\label{note:innovation_gate}
To prevent abnormal beats from corrupting the timing estimate or the repetitive template, we use the
normalized RR innovation as a gating metric:
\begin{equation}
\nu_{k+1} = \frac{z_{k+1}-\hat{T}_{k+1}}{\sqrt{P_{k+1}^-+r}}.
\end{equation}
Here $P_{k+1}^-$ is the prior RR-estimation covariance maintained by the scalar Kalman implementation.
When $|\nu_{k+1}|$ exceeds a threshold $\eta$, the beat is treated as unreliable: the R-peak predictor
and template update may be frozen, or cancellation could fall back to re-use previous R-peak anchored phase indexes.
\end{noteenv}
\begin{noteenv}\label{note:realtime_complexity}
The streaming real-time implementation of the iterative cancellation lookup and subtraction require
$O(1)$ work per sample and $O(n_t)$ memory. The scalar Kalman PI predictor will be offline calibrated or have dedicated online calibration
sessions together with possible high order IIR filters for tracking will keep the calculation cycle manageable for embedded applications.
\end{noteenv}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

To evaluate the method we implemented a robust R-peak detector and PI phase tracker for both real and
simulated data \cite{rt_eeg_proc_ekg_ilc}. We will showcase the effectiveness of the proposed real-time repetitive CFA cancellation method 
in this section.

\subsection{Real Ear-EEG Validation}
\label{sec:real_eeg_validation}
In this subsection, we use the two-channel ear-EEG configuration of the SeizeIT2 dataset, a multicenter wearable epilepsy
corpus of long-duration multimodal recordings (behind-the-ear EEG, ECG, EMG, ACC, GYR) with
focal-seizure annotations across a large cohort \cite{seizeit2}. Each EEG channel is high-pass filtered at $0.5$~Hz, notch filtered at $50$~Hz and
resampled onto the ECG sample grid, and only supervisor-approved learnable beats enter the average.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering

\caption{Buffered and strict real-time repetitive CFA cancellation on a short SeizeIT2 segment
(ds005873, sub-070, ses-01, run-20, behind-the-ear channel; 340.45--342.25~s).
Top: chest ECG (green) with buffered detected R-peaks (blue circles) and predicted
strict-real-time R-peaks (purple crosses). Bottom: original (red) and
buffered (blue) and strict real-time (purple) ear-EEG. Both outputs use the repetitive template
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:causal_ilc_update}.}
\label{fig:noncausal_cleanup}
\end{figure}
Fig.~\ref{fig:noncausal_cleanup} shows buffered and strict-real-time repetitive disturbance rejection on the
SeizeIT2 segment. The template is learned from $N_r = 851$ learnable beats over a $600$~s window; the
strict-real-time trace uses the predicted timing anchor and therefore exposes the timing-jitter penalty
relative to the buffered trace.

\emph{Template SNR via a phase-scramble null.}
Since the true disturbance $d_r$ is unobservable on real recordings, we validate the template
against a null that shares its statistics but destroys R-phase locking. Define the R-locked energy of a signal
 $y$ under
a peak set $P$ on the normalized-phase grid $\Phi$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{true}[y] \;=\; \sum_{\phi\in\Phi_{\mathrm{lobe}}}
\left[\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{k\in P} y_k(\phi)\right]^2,
\label{eq:Eop}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{\mathrm{lobe}}$ is the normalized phase grid corresponding to the main-lobe window and
$y_k(\phi)$ includes the phase denormalization or interpolation needed.

For each scramble realization
$b$, we circularly shift the complete peak train to obtain $P_{\mathrm{scr}}^{(b)}$; this preserves
the RR-interval sequence but destroys its absolute alignment with $y$, and we calculate
$\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}^{(b)}=\mathcal{E}_{P_{\mathrm{scr}}^{(b)}}[y]$. The reported null floor is
$\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}}=B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}^{(b)}$,
with $B=200$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
Q \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{true}}}{\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}}},
\qquad
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{tmpl}} = 10\log_{10} Q,
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
and a bias-corrected estimate of the CFA energy is
$\max(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{true}}-\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}},0)$.
We further define a bias-corrected CFA RMS, a scale-free measure of the artifact the template removes:
\begin{equation}
A/\mathrm{SD} = \frac{\sqrt{\max(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{true}}-\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{scr}}},0)/|\Phi_{\mathrm{lobe}}|}}{\mathrm{SD}}.
\end{equation}
The subtraction removes the leakage bias and $\mathrm{SD}=\mathrm{std}(y)$ is computed over the full
preprocessed segment, so $A/\mathrm{SD}$ is the R-locked deflection in units of EEG standard deviation.
The denominator $|\Phi_{\mathrm{lobe}}|$ is the number of phase-grid points in the lobe. For the real-data example in Fig.~\ref{fig:noncausal_cleanup}, the metric reads
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{tmpl}}=28.80$~dB and $A/\mathrm{SD}=1.061$, indicating a strong and clearly resolvable R-locked component.
Applying the same calculation to the one-beat-buffered and strict-real-time outputs gives
$-1.92$~dB/$0.000$ and $19.11$~dB/$0.328$, respectively. 
Across the listed cohort, we find 365 runs from 36 subjects exceeded $20$~dB, with mean
$\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{tmpl}}=22.67$~dB (median $22.19$~dB) and
$A/\mathrm{SD}\in[0.110,\,1.220]$ with mean $A/\mathrm{SD}=0.557$. These values were
regenerated using the raw, uncancelled EEG and 200 phase-scramble realizations per run.
Applying the same 200-scramble calculation after cancellation to all 365 runs gives a mean
buffered residual SNR of $-8.31$~dB (median $-8.29$~dB), with no runs remaining above
$20$~dB; the corresponding buffered $A/\mathrm{SD}$ has mean $0.000061$ (median $0.000$),
where the near-zero values reflect the nonnegative leakage-bias correction. For strict
real-time cancellation, the mean SNR is $17.57$~dB (median $18.20$~dB), with 110 runs above
$20$~dB, and mean $A/\mathrm{SD}=0.339$ (median $0.324$). The decrease from the raw values
indicates removal of the R-locked component; the strict-mode residual is higher because its
pre-R cancellation interval uses predicted rather than detected R-peak timing, the design of 
better R-peak predictor will be beneficial future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have presented a control-theoretic view of cardiac-field-artifact (CFA) removal in
low-channel ear-EEG: CFA is modeled as a heartbeat-phase-locked repetitive disturbance and
rejected by phase-indexed synchronous averaging and iterative learning, anchored only to
R-peak timing only without ECG waveform in streaming EEG setup. By improving SNR in the low-channel ear-EEG setting, it supports non-invasive wearable BCI
applications such as brain-state monitoring and real-time low-channel whole-brain modeling \cite{momi2025}, especially online neural feedback applications. 

More broadly, this work aims to bridge mature mechatronic disturbance-rejection theory and 
biosignal denoising, opening a control-systems avenue for wearable neural interfaces for low channel EEG real-time cleanup.
