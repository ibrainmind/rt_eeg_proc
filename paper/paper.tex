\begin{abstract}
Continuous wearable EEG is increasingly paired with consumer wearables that provide a 
heartbeat stream (e.g., smartwatch ECG/PPG). Taking the available ECG/heartbeat reference, we propose a 
repetitive-control framework that models cardiac artifact as a heartbeat-aligned periodic 
disturbance. Each EEG sample is assigned a cardiac phase index wrapped around ECG R-peaks; 
phase-synchronous averaging then preserves heartbeat-correlated residue while attenuating 
non-phase-locked noise. Subtracting this phase-indexed average yields effective artifact removal in 
the non-causal offline setting. For online real-time operation, an iterative-learning estimator is 
used to track the residue in either a same-beat predictive mode (strict causal) or a one-beat-delayed 
mode (buffered causal). We also proposes a PI based phase tracker for robust heartbeat timing estimation
from raw EKG waveforms to account for breathe related heart rate variability or immature beats rejection.
 Results on open datasets and semi-synthetic simulations show that the 
heartbeat-timing-anchored repetitive/iterative learning approach provides strong artifact suppression 
at low compute cost, making it well suited to always-on wearable execution, where blind source 
separation is typically channel-starved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Wearable EEG is moving toward continuous, day-scale monitoring, and many users already wear a second 
device that provides heartbeat timing from ECG or PPG. This creates a practical path for low-channel 
ear-EEG denoising: use heartbeat timing already available from the wearable ecosystem rather than 
requiring additional EEG channels. In ear-centered montages, cardiac field artifact (CFA) is often 
prominent because electrodes are close to neck and vascular pathways. CFA is the volume-conducted 
electrical field of the heart \cite{dirlich1997}, distinct from the scanner-related 
ballistocardiogram artifact emphasized in EEG-fMRI work \cite{niazy2005}. For out-of-
scanner earables, CFA is therefore a primary contamination mechanism.

Classical ECG-referenced adaptive cancellation is well established \cite{widrow1975}, and it is a 
natural baseline. However, in our target regime it faces two practical limits: earables are 
channel-starved for blind source separation \cite{jung2000}, and reference-to-artifact
waveform predictability can be weak under real volume-conduction mismatch and nonstationarity. Our 
real-data observations in this paper (Fig.~\ref{fig:real_aligned3beats}) support exactly this 
point: coherence between ECG and ear-EEG 
artifact is close to zero even with beat-synchronous pooling, so reference-driven waveform regression 
is limited as a primary mechanism. At the same time, beat timing remains highly informative, because 
the artifact is still phase-locked to R-peaks.

\label{fig:real_aligned3beats}

This motivates a timing-only control view: model cardiac contamination as a repetitive disturbance and 
reject it by heartbeat phase indexing. Repetitive control and iterative learning control (ILC) are 
mature tools for periodic disturbance rejection in mechatronics \cite{hara1988}, 
and they map naturally to the heartbeat cycle when robust R-peak timing is available. We therefore 
use a min/max envelope tracker with PI/PID phase tracking and arrhythmia-aware supervision to produce 
stable beat timing, then apply phase-aligned template learning/cancellation in three deployment modes: 
non-causal offline, buffered causal (one-beat latency), and strict real-time causal (zero added 
latency).

The resulting contribution is a compact, wearable-oriented CFA removal scheme aligned with realistic 
device constraints: low channel count, low compute budget, and timing references that may come from a 
separate wearable. We evaluate the design on open real recordings and semi-synthetic data, compare 
against linear RLS-based and ICA baselines, and quantify the latency-versus-suppression tradeoff 
between non-causal, buffered, and strict-causal operation.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Following repetitive-control modeling conventions \cite{hara1988,longman2000}, we model 
the measured ear-EEG signal as
\begin{equation}
y(t) = s(t) + d_r(t) + n(t),
\label{eq:meas}
\end{equation}
where $s(t)$ is the neural signal of interest, $d_r(t)$ is the heartbeat-synchronous repetitive 
cardiac disturbance, and $n(t)$ aggregates asynchronous interference and sensor noise. The subscript 
$r$ denotes the repetitive nature of the disturbance. Its period is heartbeat dependent and is set by 
ECG R-peak timing, with beat interval
\begin{equation}
T_k = t_{k+1} - t_k,
\label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
where $t_k$ is the $k$-th detected R-peak instant.

Assumption 1. In ear-EEG, CFA is dominated by the repetitive component $d_r(t)$. R-peak 
phase-asynchronous disturbances and other channel noise are represented by $n(t)$, and $n(t)$ is 
assumed zero-mean white noise. DC offset and slow drift are removed by preprocessing.

Assumption 2. The beat period $T_k$ is slowly varying, dominated by respiratory sinus arrhythmia 
(RSA), with additional small modulation from low-level muscle/motion effects in resting-state 
recordings.

Assumption 3. In the repetitive disturbance rejection and iterative learning estimator below, R-peaks 
are assumed to be accurately detected and phase-locked. A robust min/max-envelope PI tracker used for 
that locking is provided in the next section.

Using heartbeat phase, we represent the repetitive disturbance as a phase-indexed waveform
\begin{equation}
d_r(t) = d_r\!\left(\phi(t)\right), \qquad
\phi(t) = \frac{t - t_k}{T_k}, \quad t \in [t_k, t_{k+1}),
\label{eq:phase_model}
\end{equation}
with $\phi(t)\in[0,1)$ the R-peak-aligned cardiac phase. 
Equivalently, within each beat interval the disturbance is periodic in local time with period $T_k$, 
while $T_k$ itself evolves slowly across beats.
Here $t$ denotes the discrete-time index governed by the EEG sampling rate, and we assume the ECG 
reference is sampled at the same rate as EEG.

Problem 1. Finding the optimal estimate of the repetitive template $\hat{d}_r$. We assign each 
phase-aligned EEG sample a periodic phase index $\phi_p$ over an R-peak-centered template window,
\begin{subequations}
\begin{gather}
\hat{d}_r(\phi_p) = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{k=1}^{N_r} y_k(\phi_p),
\label{eq:drhat}\\
p = -n_{\mathrm{pre}},\,\ldots,\,-1,\,0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,n_{\mathrm{post}},
\label{eq:phase_index_set}\\
n_t = n_{\mathrm{pre}} + n_{\mathrm{post}},
\label{eq:Np_def}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
where $n_t$ denotes the template-window length tied to the sampling rate, $N_r$ is the number of 
R-peaks over the recording session, and $y_k(\phi_p)$ is the phase-aligned EEG sample at phase index $\phi_p$ 
from beat $k$; $p=0$ is aligned with the R-peak. Stacking $\hat{d}_r(\phi_p)$ over all $p$ yields a continuous template carried across 
all beats in the recording. The corresponding subtraction for all same-phase-synchronized EEG 
samples is
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}(t) = y(t) - \hat{d}_r\!\left(\hat{\phi}(t)\right).
\label{eq:cancel_phaseavg}
\end{equation}

Note 1. Eq.~\eqref{eq:drhat} is the non-causal phase-synchronized averaging estimate over 
the full recording, and the resulting template is used for subtraction in 
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cancel_phaseavg}.

Note 2. Based on Assumption 1, in the Eq.~\eqref{eq:drhat} estimate, $n(t)$ is rendered 
toward zero as the sample mean of zero-mean white noise.

Note 3. A practical guideline for choosing $n_{\mathrm{pre}}$, $n_{\mathrm{post}}$, and $n_t$: 
set $n_{\mathrm{pre}} = \lfloor 0.05\,f_s \rfloor$ (about 50~ms before the R-peak, covering 
QRS onset) and $n_{\mathrm{post}} = \lfloor 0.55\,f_s \rfloor$ (about 550~ms after, covering 
the full QRS-T complex at resting heart rate), giving $n_t = n_{\mathrm{pre}} + n_{\mathrm{post}} 
\approx 0.6\,f_s$ samples, i.e.\ 600~ms total window length. At $f_s = 250$~Hz this yields 
$n_t = 150$ samples.

Problem 2. Estimating beat timing robustly, i.e., the R-peak index $k$ and R-peak instant, so 
that EEG samples can be phase-aligned. We denote estimated R-peaks by $\hat{t}_k$, estimated 
beat period by $\hat{T}_k=\hat{t}_{k+1}-\hat{t}_k$, and estimated phase by
\begin{equation}
\hat{\phi}(t) = \frac{t-\hat{t}_k}{\hat{T}_k}, \qquad t\in[\hat{t}_k,\hat{t}_{k+1}).
\label{eq:phi_hat}
\end{equation}
The next section addresses this timing-estimation problem with a min/max-envelope PI tracker.

Note 4. Since $T_k$ is slowly varying (Assumption 2), $\hat{T}_k$ can be reliably predicted 
between successive R-peaks. The PI tracker operates as a phase-locked loop (PLL): it uses 
detected R-peak fiducials to correct the instantaneous rate estimate and advances phase 
continuously between beats, so that $\hat{\phi}(t)$ remains well-defined even in real-time 
causal operation where the next R-peak has not yet been observed.

\section{Repetitive Disturbance Rejection}
Given the phase-indexed disturbance model in Sec.~II, we perform cancellation by repetitive 
disturbance rejection with iterative learning on heartbeat-aligned samples. The cancellation target 
is the beat-synchronous component of CFA, while asynchronous components are left in the residual.

\subsection{ILC/repetitive template cancellation}
A template $m(o)$, indexed by time-since-R offset $o$, stores $d_p$. Cancellation is a table lookup 
at the current offset,
\begin{equation}
e_I(t) = y(t) - m(o(t)),
\label{eq:ilccancel}
\end{equation}
and learning is a first-order ILC (leaky-integrator) update,
\begin{equation}
m(o) \leftarrow m(o) + a \, e_I(t),
\label{eq:ilclearn}
\end{equation}
applied only on normal beats, with $a$ the ILC learning gain. Because we estimate an additive 
disturbance rather than invert a plant, the effective plant is the identity, and the ILC 
convergence condition reduces to $|q(1-a)| < 1$ for a $Q$-filter/forgetting factor 
$q\in(0,1]$---trivially satisfied for any sane $a$. The factor $q$ trades asymptotic cancellation 
for drift tracking.

Detection latency forces a mode choice: the R-peak is confirmed $\sim$60--80~ms after it occurs, so 
\emph{buffered} operation ($\approx$1-beat latency) anchors each sample to its detected $t_k$ and 
removes the sharp R spike fully, whereas \emph{causal} (zero-latency) operation must anchor to the 
PLL-predicted R-time; the R-spike depth is then capped by RR predictability (RSA-lag dominated). 
The broad P and T waves survive prediction; only the R spike is latency-critical.

\subsection{Safeguards against cancellation of neural signal}
\label{sec:safeguards}
Because $s(t)$ can share frequency content with $d(t)$, frequency alone cannot separate them; the 
synthesis instead relies on \emph{phase-lock} and \emph{reference-correlation}, together with two 
explicit limiters.

\emph{Phase-lock gate (structural).} The ILC template \eqref{eq:ilclearn} only accumulates energy 
that is consistent across many beats at the same offset $o$---it is structurally an evoked average. 
EEG activity that is not phase-locked to the R-peak averages toward zero in $m(o)$ rather than 
being learned, regardless of its frequency. Cancellation \eqref{eq:ilccancel} is further confined 
to samples inside the template window $o(t)\in[0,L_w)$; outside it, $y(t)$ is untouched.

\emph{Amplitude limiter.} To bound worst-case over-subtraction, cancellation is capped using the 
already-computed peak tracker $pk(t)$ from \eqref{eq:gate}:
\begin{equation}
e_I(t) = y(t) - \mathrm{sat}_{\pm\beta\, pk(t)}\big(m(o(t))\big), \quad \beta \in (0,1),
\label{eq:limiter}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sat}_{\pm L}(x) = \max(-L,\min(L,x))$. The subtracted amount can never exceed a 
fraction $\beta$ of the tracked ECG amplitude, a physically motivated ceiling on plausible CFA 
magnitude.

\emph{Remaining risk.} The heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP) is genuine neural activity that 
\emph{is} phase-locked to the heartbeat \cite{park2019}; phase-lock alone cannot distinguish it 
from CFA, and it is only partially protected by \eqref{eq:limiter}. We report 
alpha-rhythm preservation (Sec.~VII) as an indirect check; an explicit HEP-preservation test is 
future work.

\section{ECG Phase Estimator}

\subsection{Min/max tracker + PI/PID phase tracking}
A QRS-emphasis band-pass feeds two streaming trackers: a peak tracker $pk(t)$ (fast attack, slow 
release) and a baseline tracker $bl(t)$ (slow moving average). The normalized gate
\begin{equation}
g(t) = \frac{x(t) - bl(t)}{pk(t) - bl(t)}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
is amplitude-drift invariant, so posture/contact drift does not defeat detection. A Schmitt 
hysteresis and a 250~ms refractory yield clean beat gating; the fiducial $t_k$ is the raw-reference 
maximum within the gated window (low jitter).

A PI phase-locked loop converts sparse fiducials into a continuous phase. Between beats the phase 
advances by the instantaneous rate estimate,
\begin{equation}
p(t+1) = \big[\, p(t) + \mathrm{fr}(t) \,\big] \bmod 1, \qquad \mathrm{fr}(t) = 
\frac{1}{\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}(t)\, f_s},
\label{eq:phaseacc}
\end{equation}
where $f_s$ is the sample rate. At each fiducial, a proportional term realigns phase and an 
integral term updates the rate estimate,
\begin{align}
p &\leftarrow p - K_p \, e_p, \label{eq:pcorr}\\
\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}} &\leftarrow \mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}} + K_i 
\,(\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meas}} - \mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}), \label{eq:rcorr}
\end{align}
with $e_p$ the wrapped phase error and $K_p,K_i$ the PI gains. The loop bandwidth is set above 
respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA, $\sim$0.2--0.4~Hz) and below per-beat detection noise. An 
arrhythmia supervisor classifies each beat by the ratio 
$\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meas}}/\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{est}}$ and applies the matching 
reflex---\emph{premature}: trust the measured phase, freeze the rate and learning; \emph{missed}: 
trust the prediction, keep the rate---protecting both the loop and the template.

\subsection{Algorithm summary}
Per sample: (1)~update trackers \eqref{eq:gate} and gate; (2)~advance phase \eqref{eq:phaseacc}, 
correct at fiducials \eqref{eq:pcorr}--\eqref{eq:rcorr}, run the supervisor; (3)~ILC 
lookup/limited-subtract \eqref{eq:ilccancel},\eqref{eq:limiter} (update \eqref{eq:ilclearn} on 
normal beats). The proposed online path is therefore constant-time per sample, with template memory 
linear in the window length.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Complexity Analysis}
Let $L_w$ be the template length and $C$ the channel count. The proposed phase-tracker + ILC path 
is lightweight: tracker updates and gating are constant-time, PI/PID phase update is constant-time 
(amortized), and ILC lookup/update is constant-time with memory $L_w$. Thus, per-sample complexity 
is $O(1)$ and memory is $O(L_w)$ for each channel. This profile is suitable for always-on 
low-power ear-EEG devices. For fairness, baseline adaptive cancellers (e.g., LMS/RLS) are reported 
separately in experiments and are not part of the proposed online deployment path.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Real ear-EEG validation: coherence and repeatable-template evidence}
\label{sec:realdata}
To test the coherence assumption behind reference-driven cancellers and confirm that a genuine
repeatable cardiac template exists outside simulation, we analyze a 10-minute segment (717 beats;
2 premature, 0 missed) from the SeizeIT2 dataset \cite{seizeit2}, which pairs a wearable
behind-the-ear EEG channel (256~Hz) with a synchronized chest ECG lead during video-EEG epilepsy
monitoring.

\emph{Coherence is near zero, even under best-case R-locked pooling.} Fig.~\ref{fig:coh}(a) shows
magnitude-squared coherence between the ECG reference and the ear-EEG channel from long, many-average
Welch segments across 0--60~Hz: coherence averages $6\times10^{-10}$ over 8--20~Hz and is visually
indistinguishable from zero across the whole spectrum -- inconsistent with any stationary linear
channel between $r(t)$ and $d(t)$. A skeptic could object that this washes out a relationship
holding only locally around each beat; pooling short-time estimates over rolling 12-beat R-aligned
batches (715 estimates, Fig.~\ref{fig:coh}(b)) gives the same answer -- coherence stays low and
stable ($0.06$--$0.17$, mean $0.11$) throughout the recording, an order of magnitude below what a
usable Hammerstein/RLS reference-driven canceller would need. Even granting the reference every
possible timing advantage, the ear-EEG artifact waveform is not linearly predictable from the ECG
waveform.

\emph{A repeatable, R-anchored template nonetheless exists.} Coherence measures waveform
predictability from the reference, not repeatability across beats. Non-causal ILC/synchronous
averaging (\S IV-B) applied directly to the real ear-EEG channel (Fig.~\ref{fig:real_causal_buffered})
recovers a clear, consistent deflection time-locked to the ECG R-peak, confirming the cardiac field
artifact at the ear is genuinely timing-repeatable -- exactly the property ILC exploits -- even
though it fails the coherence test above. This is the real-data analogue of synchronous-average
disk-servo RRO/NRRO separation: only the R-peak \emph{instant} is used, never the ECG waveform shape.
The causal-vs-buffered R-spike tradeoff is likewise real, not a simulation artifact: buffered matches
non-causal (R-spike peak-to-peak $5.02\times10^{-5}$~V vs.\ $5.07\times10^{-5}$~V), while the causal
template is capped by RR-prediction error to $3.47\times10^{-5}$~V, a $32\%$ reduction, consistent
with \S IV-B. Cardiac-band (1--20~Hz) power is reduced by $0.9$/$0.8$/$0.4$~dB for
non-causal/buffered/causal respectively; the modest magnitude versus the semi-synthetic ceiling
(Table~\ref{tab:results}) reflects broadband physiological noise at this electrode and that only the
beat-locked component is addressable by ILC by construction.

These observations are the empirical basis for this paper's scope: a null coherence result that
rules out a reference-driven residual stage as a primary contribution here, and a positive
repeatability result that justifies keeping the timing-driven ILC stage. The linear RLS canceller in
Table~\ref{tab:results} is retained only as a semi-synthetic baseline, evaluated on data explicitly
given a known Hammerstein/volume-conduction channel between $r(t)$ and $d(t)$; it is not part of the
proposed real-time deployment path.

\subsection{Semi-synthetic data}
The real-data evidence above has no ground truth $s(t)$, so we complement it with a semi-synthetic
protocol that provides exact-reference SNR metrics; the ILC-driven design validated qualitatively
above is here validated quantitatively. Following the semi-synthetic protocol common in EEG denoising \cite{zhang2021}, a low-CFA neural
surrogate (pink noise plus an amplitude-modulated 10~Hz alpha rhythm) provides ground truth $s(t)$. 
A synthetic ECG source $u(t)$ with RSA-modulated RR intervals is converted into a beat-locked 
artifact template with slow amplitude and morphology drift, then added at a controlled input SNR; 
one premature and one dropped beat are injected. This yields exact-reference metrics without manual 
labeling. Metrics: artifact SNR (steady state), spectral cardiac-peak reduction, and alpha 
preservation.

\subsection{Baselines}
Within the semi-synthetic protocol only (\S\ref{sec:realdata} shows why a reference-driven residual
stage cannot contribute on the real recording), we compare against (i)~a linear RLS/LMS
ECG-reference canceller \cite{widrow1975,haykin2014,sayed2008}; (ii)~an EKF/state-space canceller
\emph{[placeholder---to be added]} \cite{sameni2007}; and (iii)~low-channel ICA
\emph{[placeholder---to be added]} \cite{jung2000}, which is expected to degrade at $C<8$.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports steady-state artifact SNR for the main approaches, generated 
directly by the companion script to keep manuscript numbers synchronized with code. 
Fig.~\ref{fig:conv} shows single-pass online convergence for the three operating modes and the 
corresponding buffered-mode spectrum before and after cleaning. In single-pass online operation, 
buffered mode converges faster and to a higher final SNR than causal mode, consistent with the 
expected one-beat anchoring advantage. Spectrally, the cardiac comb is strongly reduced after 
cleaning while the 10~Hz alpha peak is preserved within about 10\%, an implicit 
heartbeat-evoked-potential safeguard \cite{park2019}. Ablations show that phase anchoring quality 
and mode selection (non-causal vs buffered vs strict-causal) dominate performance: buffered ILC is 
closest to non-causal performance, while strict-causal ILC is limited by RR prediction accuracy 
around sharp R-wave transitions.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a control-theoretic synthesis---min/max-tracker PI-PLL phase estimation,
ILC/repetitive template cancellation, and explicit non-causal/buffered/strict-causal operating
modes---for real-time CFA removal in low-channel ear-EEG. On a real behind-the-ear EEG/chest-ECG
recording, heartbeat-locked coherence between the two is near zero even under best-case R-locked
pooling, while R-anchored synchronous averaging still recovers a clear, repeatable template and
reproduces the causal-vs-buffered R-spike tradeoff quantitatively -- evidence that, at least on this
data, timing-only ILC is both necessary (reference-driven waveform regression has nothing to
regress against) and sufficient (the artifact is genuinely timing-repeatable). On the semi-synthetic
protocol, the method exceeds linear ANC at equal latency, exposes a quantified one-beat-latency
vs.\ zero-latency trade-off set by RR predictability, preserves the alpha rhythm, and runs at
constant per-sample cost suitable for edge deployment.

More broadly, this work aims to bridge mature mechatronic disturbance-rejection theory and 
biosignal denoising, opening a control-systems avenue for wearable neural interfaces. The 
disturbance-observer perspective extends naturally beyond the cardiac artifact: an inertial 
(gyroscope/accelerometer) reference could drive feed-forward cancellation of motion artifact, and 
an event-driven detector could trigger transient cancellation of eye-blink or jaw-clench artifacts, 
suggesting a unified, reference-driven, control-based artifact-rejection subsystem for earable EEG. 
Future work includes validation on recorded ear-EEG, multichannel spatial coupling for $C\le8$, an 
RSA-aware R predictor to lift the zero-latency R-spike ceiling, and an algorithm-unrolled low-power 
realization.
